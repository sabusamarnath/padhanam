# A Decision Intelligence Framework for a Senior Job Search

The earlier version of this model described how to run one process well: a linear flow of stages, each preceded by a decision rhombus, each rhombus powered by a local causal decision diagram of levers, externals, intermediaries, outcomes, and a local goal. That engine still holds. What it lacked was the layer above the flow and the loop around it.

This version fixes three properties. The model was linear, so it ignored that your real leverage comes from running several processes at once. It was open-loop, so conversion data never fed back into upstream levers. It was static, so timing, which is what turns a pipeline into negotiating power, had no representation. The framework below is built in three layers: a portfolio layer that sits on top, a process layer that runs each opportunity, and a set of state variables that flow through both.

---

## 1. Architecture

The search is a timed portfolio of parallel processes, instrumented and revised as it runs.

- The portfolio layer is the primary control system. It decides how many processes to run, where preparation goes, when to start and kill, and how to synchronize timing so options mature together.
- The process layer is the gated flow for a single opportunity. Each gate is a rhombus resolved by its local CDD.
- The state variables (leverage, information, capacity, and the multi-objective goal) are not events. They accumulate and flow through every gate and are spent or realized at the end.

For someone at your level the role is rarely won inside one funnel. It is won by managing several funnels against the clock, measuring them, and converting that structure into leverage.

---

## 2. The portfolio layer

This is the layer the original diagram could not draw, because the decision that matters most is not which gate to pass but how to run the set.

Parallelism is the control, not a footnote. The questions are how many processes to keep live, how to allocate finite preparation across them, when to open new ones, and when to kill weak ones to protect the strong. Treat the pipeline as a portfolio with a target depth, not as a queue you work one at a time.

Synchronization is how parallelism becomes leverage. Processes move at different speeds, and leverage exists only when a second strong option matures inside the decision window of the first. An offer that expires Friday is worthless against a final round the following Tuesday. The portfolio layer actively paces: slowing fast processes with scheduling and considered responses, accelerating slow ones by pushing for compressed timelines, and deliberately bunching final stages so offers land close together. Leverage at the Negotiate gate is manufactured here, weeks earlier, on the calendar.

The feedback loop is what makes this decision intelligence rather than a flowchart. Conversion data revises upstream levers. A low application-to-screen rate is a signal to change targeting or channel, not to apply harder. Clearing CV rounds but failing cases is a signal to drill case reps, not to widen the funnel. The loop only exists if you instrument it. See section 6 for the metrics.

Capacity is a hard global constraint. Energy, time, and morale are finite, and long processes invite sunk-cost traps. The portfolio has a budget. When you are running more processes than you can run well, the correct move is to cut, not to push through.

Portfolio-level gates that sit outside any single process:

- Start a new process. Fire when pipeline depth drops below target or when no live process is strong. The lever is inbound and outbound origination.
- Kill a process. Fire when a process consumes preparation that a stronger one needs, or when its probability-adjusted value falls below the opportunity cost. Withdrawing well preserves the relationship.
- Offer comparison. The terminal decision is rarely accept or reject in isolation. It is choosing among parallel offers, a different and harder evaluation than judging one alone. This gate only exists if synchronization produced more than one live offer.

## 3. The process layer

The full sequence for a single opportunity. Stages in brackets are modular: plug in the ones a given process actually uses and drop the rest. Each gate carries its local CDD.

### Pre-search gate (whether to move at all)

- Decision: open a search, or stay and improve the current role.
- Levers: defining your walk-away, internal repositioning, clarifying what would have to be true to move.
- Externals: current-role trajectory, market timing, personal constraints.
- Intermediary: expected uplift from moving versus staying.
- Outcomes: a justified search, or a decision to stay that is itself a win.
- Local goal: confirm that moving beats the status quo. Staying is your true BATNA and a legitimate goal-satisfying outcome.

### Target?

- Decision: which segments, levels, and companies to pursue.
- Levers: skill positioning, geographic and remote scope, seniority aim.
- Externals: labor market demand, hiring concentration by sector.
- Intermediary: size and quality of the addressable role pool.
- Outcomes: expected volume of viable roles per week.
- Local goal: a target list deep enough to sustain a portfolio.

### Search

The process stage. Output feeds the Apply gate.

### Apply?

- Decision: apply tailored, apply quick, or skip.
- Levers: tailoring effort, referral pursuit, time budget, recruiter as advocate.
- Externals: JD match, applicant pool size, referral availability, whether the role is wired for an internal candidate.
- Intermediary: estimated screen-through probability as fit times conversion likelihood.
- Outcomes: expected interviews generated versus the opportunity cost of the same effort elsewhere.
- Local goal: highest return on marginal effort.

### Application

The process stage. Submission.

### Screening and assessment gate

A distinct stage between application and interviews, with its own conversion rate and its own drop-off.

- Decision: invest in the take-home or assessment, or decline.
- Levers: quality and time spent on take-homes, assessment preparation, clarifying expectations with the recruiter.
- Externals: recruiter screen, take-home design, online assessment cut-offs.
- Intermediary: probability of clearing to first interview.
- Outcomes: advance versus screened out, plus early intel on band and process.
- Local goal: clear the gate efficiently without over-investing before signal exists.

### Interview block (modular, plug and play)

The Pursue? gate fires once before each plugged-in stage and is re-evaluated with the previous round's information, so the decision can flip mid-process. Assemble per company.

Intro
- Tests: baseline fit, motivation, logistics. A cheap filter.
- Levers: tight 60-second narrative, early comp anchoring, signals of interest, qualifying questions.
- Externals: recruiter brief, hard must-haves, comp band.
- Intermediary: perceived baseline fit and mutual interest.
- Outcomes: advance versus screened out, plus process and budget intel.
- Local goal: clear the filter and qualify the opportunity.

CV interview
- Tests: the substance behind the CV. Ownership, results, claims under scrutiny.
- Levers: quantified impact stories, role-specific framing, pre-emptive handling of gaps.
- Externals: hiring manager priorities, competencies probed, strength of the comparison set.
- Intermediary: assessed credibility of your track record.
- Outcomes: advance with a strong competence signal versus flagged concerns.
- Local goal: convert CV claims into evidence-backed ownership.

Case interview
- Tests: structured problem-solving and commercial judgment under time pressure.
- Levers: a clear structuring approach, hypothesis-first communication, numerical fluency, reps.
- Externals: case design, evaluator rubric, difficulty calibration.
- Intermediary: perceived analytical ability.
- Outcomes: advance versus concerns on the analytical axis.
- Local goal: demonstrate structured thinking and sound judgment under observation.

Group interview
- Tests: behavior around other strong people. Collaboration, influence, operating in a room you do not control.
- Levers: calibrated assertiveness, building on others, visible-but-not-dominant contribution, listening signals.
- Externals: group composition, evaluator rubric, the dynamic other candidates set.
- Intermediary: perceived interpersonal and working-style fit.
- Outcomes: advance versus concerns on the collaboration axis.
- Local goal: demonstrate influence without dominance.

Meet the team
- Tests: mutual fit and chemistry with future peers. Often a two-way sell and a soft veto point.
- Levers: rapport, thoughtful role and team questions, consistency with earlier rounds, genuine interest in their work.
- Externals: team dynamics, who is in the room, unspoken concerns carried over.
- Intermediary: perceived cultural and peer fit.
- Outcomes: positive team signal versus a quiet flag, plus your own read on the team.
- Local goal: secure peer buy-in and pressure-test your own fit.

CxO / Founder
- Tests: strategic alignment, trust, conviction. Competence is largely settled; this is about whether leadership wants you in the room.
- Levers: a strategic narrative tied to their priorities, senior-signaling questions, visible conviction, quiet leverage from other live processes.
- Externals: founder chemistry, company stage and urgency, board or budget constraints.
- Intermediary: perceived strategic fit and leadership conviction.
- Outcomes: an offer and its strength, versus no offer.
- Local goal: establish mutual conviction and begin building offer leverage.

### References and background gate

Late in the process. It rarely changes the outcome but it can derail one.

- Decision: which references to put forward and how to prime them.
- Levers: reference selection, briefing them on the role and the narrative, sequencing.
- Externals: background-check scope, a weak relationship from a past role surfacing.
- Intermediary: consistency between your story and external validation.
- Outcomes: clean confirmation versus a flag that reopens doubt.
- Local goal: remove the last source of downside risk.

### Negotiate?

- Decision: accept as-is, negotiate hard, or negotiate selectively.
- Levers: anchor, counter structure, which dimensions to prioritize, use of competing processes.
- Externals: comp bands, market-rate data, the strength of competing offers, urgency.
- Intermediary: estimated negotiation surplus and rescind risk.
- Outcomes: improved package versus relationship and risk cost.
- Local goal: capture available surplus without jeopardizing the offer.

### Offer

The process stage. The offer is multi-dimensional, not a single comp figure. Every dimension carries value and most are negotiable: base, bonus, equity structure, title, scope, mandate, team, start date, and notice terms. Senior offers are won and lost on scope and mandate as much as on cash.

### Accept? (resolved against the offer comparison gate)

- Decision: accept, reject, or stall for a maturing option.
- Levers: decision-criteria weighting, BATNA, timeline management.
- Externals: other offers in hand, deadline pressure.
- Intermediary: each offer scored against your weighted, multi-objective criteria.
- Outcomes: realized value of accepting now versus waiting.
- Local goal: select the offer that maximizes the multi-objective goal.

### Role secured

The achieved goal.

## 4. State variables that flow through the system

These are not gate-local. They accumulate across the whole pipeline.

Leverage is an accumulating state, not a switch at the Negotiate gate. It is built from the Apply stage onward through competing processes, a strong internal champion, demonstrated conviction in late rounds, and intel gathered along the way. It flows through the pipeline and is spent once, at the end. The candidates who negotiate from strength treated the founder round as the start of the negotiation.

Information is a compounding asset, not a byproduct. The band you learn at Intro, the criteria you infer at the CV round, the identity of the real decision-maker, and the unspoken concerns you detect all feed forward into Negotiate and Accept. Track intel as a deliberate output of every interaction.

Win probability is a per-process state that updates as gates are passed. Each gate is an information event, not just a hurdle: clearing it revises the conditional probability of an offer from that specific process upward. The funnel is therefore a Bayesian updating process, and the posterior is the quantity the portfolio layer allocates against. As a process advances and its probability rises, preparation and synchronization effort should shift toward it. The same updates sharpen the Pursue and kill gates, whose real input is revised probability times offer value against opportunity cost. Two cautions hold. The signal is probability of an offer, not probability of having already invested, so a rising posterior does not license sunk-cost persistence. And because probability compounds late in the funnel, the marginal value of preparation rises through it, which is the formal reason the discriminator and sell rounds deserve the heaviest effort.

Estimating this probability is the hard part, because your own effort is an input, not the output, and the offer is decided by externals you cannot see: the committee debrief, an internal candidate, a frozen budget. Measuring your activity feels like signal but measures the wrong quantity. The correction is to measure their revealed effort toward you instead, which is the observable shadow of their hidden conviction: response speed, the seniority of who they route you to next, the conversation flipping from testing you to selling you and describing what you would own, and unprompted specifics on scope, comp, or start date. Generic warmth, politeness, and your own felt confidence are free to give and carry no information. The estimation method follows from this. Anchor on the base rate of offers at each stage rather than gut feel, update only on the costly signals, and use intel deliberately to convert invisible externals into visible ones, since each fact extracted shrinks the part of the probability you are guessing at. Calibrate over many processes by recording the estimate against the outcome; the gap is your bias, and it runs optimistic on processes you have invested in. The honest output is a band, not a number, and that irreducibility is itself the formal case for portfolio breadth: the search is made robust by the count of live processes, not by confidence in any one.

Capacity is a finite global budget across energy, time, and morale. It binds the whole portfolio. When capacity is the constraint, the resolution is at the portfolio layer: kill a process, not work harder.

The goal is multi-objective. Best-fit role is one axis. You are also optimizing optionality, your reputation with people you may work with or for again, and the relationships the search itself builds. A process you decline well still carries positive value. Define the weights up front so the Accept gate has a scoring function rather than a gut feel.

## 5. Instrumentation: the metrics that close the loop

The diagnostic power of the model only exists if you measure the conversions. Track each rate and read the lever it points to.

- Application to screen. Low rate points to targeting or channel, not volume. Shift cold applications to warm.
- Screen to first interview. Low rate points to positioning and the recruiter relationship.
- Round to round within the interview block. Persistent failure at one stage type isolates the lever: case reps, story specificity, or interpersonal calibration.
- Final round to offer. Low rate points to conviction and fit in the sell rounds.
- Offer to accept. A low rate is not always bad; it can mean strong optionality. Read it against your goal weights.
- Pipeline depth and offer synchronization. The portfolio-level metrics. Depth protects against single-process failure; synchronization produces the leverage.

Without these numbers the levers are untestable assertions.

## 6. The functional taxonomy

Regardless of order, every interview stage falls into one of three types, and preparation should be allocated by type, not by count.

- Filters remove people. Intro, CV, and the screening gate. The objective is not to lose. Over-preparing here wastes effort.
- Discriminators rank people. Case and Group. This is where preparation has the highest marginal return for a senior candidate, because these rounds separate rather than cut.
- Fit-and-sell rounds build conviction and leverage. Meet the team and CxO / Founder. These pre-load the Negotiate and Accept gates.

Equal preparation across all stages is the common senior-candidate mistake. It overspends on filters and underinvests in the rounds that rank and sell you.

---

## 7. Underweighted nodes to add to your tracking

Levers you control but may not be naming: inbound and personal brand that make roles come to you and shrink the Apply funnel; the recruiter as an internal agent and advocate; deliberate creation of competitive tension; and the active, credible use of walk-away.

Externals you should be tracking but may not be: a role effectively wired for an internal or referred candidate; turnover of your champion mid-process; a mid-process hiring freeze; and the debrief and committee dynamics decided by people you never meet.

---

## The through-line

What you originally built describes how to run one process well. This version adds the system that runs the whole search as a timed portfolio with a feedback loop, with leverage and information treated as state that compounds across the pipeline. Build the layer above the flow, instrument the loop around it, and your existing gate-level CDDs become the engine that layer drives.
